Why Fall Is the Best Time to Take Down a Tree in the Boundary

August 22, 2026

Every winter, usually somewhere around the first heavy snowfall, we get a version of the same phone call. There is a tree — the caller has been keeping an eye on it since spring, meaning to do something about it — and last night half of it came down on the fence, or the woodshed, or the line to the shop. Now it is an emergency job, in the dark, in the snow, at the end of a driveway that is no longer really a driveway.

Nobody plans it that way. But every one of those calls started out as a tree somebody noticed in August and filed under "later." So this one is for the August version of you, while later is still on the table: fall is the best time of year to take down a tree in the Boundary, and it is not close.

The summer just showed you which trees are in trouble

Late summer is when a struggling tree stops hiding it. A fir or pine that spent June and July quietly shutting down will be declaring it by now — a thin, see-through crown, a dead top, needles browning from the inside of the branch outward, leaves letting go weeks ahead of everything around them. We wrote about what back-to-back dry years do to Boundary trees, and the short version is that the damage accumulates quietly and then shows up all at once, usually about now.

That makes late August and September the honest audit window. Walk your property and look up. If something looks wrong, compare it against the five warning signs a tree needs to come down — and if it ticks more than one box, this fall is the time to deal with it, not next spring.

Why fall makes the work easier — and easier on your property

  • The leaves come off, and the truth comes out. Once a deciduous tree drops its leaves, the structure is right there to see — cracks, cavities, deadwood, the lean you could only half-convince yourself about in July. Bare crowns are also lighter and simpler to rig, which means smaller drop zones and less of the tree passing over your roof on a rope.
  • The birds are done with it. Songbird nesting season wraps up by late summer, so there is no risk of opening up a crown and finding an active nest that stops the job. In fall, a removal is just a removal.
  • The ground can take the equipment. A chipper, a truck, and a few tonnes of wood have to get across your yard somehow. On firm, dry fall ground they leave barely a mark. The same job in spring, on saturated lawn, is a rut-repair project we would both rather skip.
  • The debris has somewhere to go. We chip most of what comes down, but if your plan for the rest involves a burn pile, fall is when open-burning restrictions start easing after fire season. And a tree dropped in October is seasoned firewood by the time you actually want it.
  • The schedule still has room. Fall work gets booked at our pace and yours — you pick the week. Winter storm cleanup gets booked at the storm's pace, and the storm does not consult anyone.

The alternative is letting a storm pick the date

A compromised tree that stands through September does not get a pass on winter. It gets wet snow loading on a weakened crown, then wind on top of that, then a freeze-thaw cycle working on every crack the summer opened up. The trees that come down in winter storms are overwhelmingly the ones that were already telegraphing it in August.

We have written before about what waiting too long on a danger tree actually costs, so we will not repeat the whole grim arithmetic here. The summary: a planned fall removal is one tidy line on a quote. A tree on your roof in January is an emergency callout, a tarp, a repair bill, and an insurance conversation — and we answer that phone 24/7, but nobody's budget prefers it.

Chasing the FireSmart rebate? Watch the calendar

One more reason this fall matters more than most: the RDKB's FireSmart rebate — up to $5,000 back on qualifying wildfire-mitigation work, including conifer removal and thinning near the house — takes applications until October 31, 2026, first-come, first-served while the funding lasts. The catch is that the free FireSmart assessment has to happen before the work does. If that is news to you, read our full breakdown of how the rebate works, then book the assessment now so there is still time to do the work and file the paperwork behind it.

What to do this week

Walk the property. Look up. Make a short list of the trees you have been wondering about — the dead top by the driveway, the fir leaning a little more than it used to, the one the last windstorm left creaking. Then have us out for a free quote. Not everything on your list will need to come down; some of it just needs pruning at the right time, and we will tell you which is which.

But do it while it is still a fall booking — a date on a calendar, dry ground, clear sky, tidy stack of wood. The winter version of the same tree is a much worse phone call, and we would rather meet your tree on your schedule than on the storm's.
